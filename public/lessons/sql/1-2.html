Filtering with WHERE

Most questions are about some rows, not all of them. WHERE keeps only the rows that match a rule. This is the keyword you will type most often in your whole career.

25 min Beginner Practice: 6 questions

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Keep only matching rows with WHERE
  • Compare numbers, text and dates with =, <>, >, <
  • Combine rules with AND, OR, NOT and brackets
  • Use the shortcuts IN, BETWEEN and LIKE

Definition

Key definition

WHERE is a rule tested on every row. A row that passes the rule stays in the result. A row that fails is left out.

The rule is called a condition. It is a tiny yes-or-no question such as “is the city Mumbai?” or “is the balance above 5 lakh?”. SQL asks that question of every row, one at a time, and keeps the rows that answer yes.

WHERE filters rows. SELECT picks columns. Keep those two jobs apart in your head and queries become easy to read.

Think of it like a sticky note on the request slip

You hand the librarian the usual slip: “copy name and city from the customers shelf”. Now you add a sticky note: “only cards where the city says Mumbai”. She still picks up every card on the shelf, but she only copies the ones that match the note. The rest go straight back. The shelf itself is untouched.

In the libraryThe sticky noteIn SQL: the WHERE clause
In the library“city says Mumbai”In SQL: a condition, city = 'Mumbai'
In the libraryTwo notes joined by “and”In SQL: AND (both must be true) or OR (either is enough)

A good sticky note is exact. “Big balance” means nothing to the librarian. “balance above 500000” she can test on every card.

How to write it, step by step

Step 1: write the normal SELECT and FROM. Step 2: add WHERE and one condition. Step 3: join more conditions with AND or OR.

SELECT cust_name, city, segment
FROM customers
WHERE city = 'Mumbai' AND segment = 'PREMIUM';

Three rules for values. Text goes in single quotes and is case-sensitive: 'Mumbai' is not 'mumbai'. Numbers have no quotes: balance > 500000. Dates are written as text in the form 'YYYY-MM-DD': txn_date >= '2026-09-01'.

Brackets decide the order. AND is tested before OR, so a OR b AND c means a OR (b AND c). When you mix them, always add brackets so the slip says exactly what you mean.

Common mistakes

Watch out

1. WHERE city = 'mumbai' returns nothing. Text matching is exact, including capital letters.

2. WHERE city = 'Delhi' OR 'Pune' is an error. Each side of OR is a full condition: city = 'Delhi' OR city = 'Pune', or shorter, city IN ('Delhi', 'Pune').

3. segment = 'RETAIL' AND city = 'Delhi' OR city = 'Pune' gives every Pune customer, retail or not. AND wins over OR. Add brackets.

4. WHERE age = NULL never matches anything. A blank field needs IS NULL. Lesson 1.5 explains why.

5. LIKE 'a%' does not find Asha. Patterns are case-sensitive too. Use LIKE 'A%', or see the Snowflake note below.

On Snowflake

What is different on your project

ILIKE ignores case. Snowflake has cust_name ILIKE 'a%', which matches Asha, Arjun and Ananya. The worksheet engine (DuckDB) supports it too. The portable way is UPPER(cust_name) LIKE 'A%', which you learn in lesson 1.4.

Date literals. txn_date >= '2026-09-01' works in both. The explicit form DATE '2026-09-01' also works in both and makes your intent obvious to a reader.

Values are still case-sensitive. Table and column names are not case-sensitive in Snowflake, but the text inside a column is. 'Mumbai' and 'MUMBAI' are different values on both engines.

Practice

Same bank, now all three tables: customers, accounts and transactions (24 rows of money moving in September 2026 and the last days of August). Each question names the columns to return. The checker compares values only.

Key takeaway

Remember

WHERE is a yes-or-no rule tested on every row. Text in single quotes and exact case, numbers bare, dates as 'YYYY-MM-DD'. Brackets whenever AND meets OR.

Glossary

Condition
A yes-or-no test on one row, such as balance > 500000.
Filter
Keeping only the rows that pass a condition. WHERE is the filter.
Operator
A comparison symbol or word: =, <>, >, IN, BETWEEN, LIKE.
Literal
A fixed value typed into the query, such as 'Mumbai' or 500000.
Wildcard
In LIKE, % stands for any text and _ for one character.
Precedence
The order rules are tested: NOT first, then AND, then OR. Brackets override it.
